For more than two hundred years, the Old Stone Mill has stood at the head of Lower Beverley Lake, its limestone walls grinding the wheat of the surrounding countryside into flour. This is its story — gathered from ledgers, censuses, photographs and recollection.

It is rare, in a country still measured in centuries rather than millennia, to find a working artefact that has survived the passage of two hundred years more or less intact. The Old Stone Mill is such a thing. Built in the years before the War of 1812 by a partnership of Loyalist millwrights and local subscribers, it has milled grain for the farmers of the upper Rideau watershed under the names of a half-dozen owners, weathered fire, frost, and the silent indignities of disuse, and emerged — restored, interpreted, and once again grinding flour — as the oldest pre-1812 stone gristmill still standing in the Province of Ontario.

What follows is not a definitive history. It is, instead, a curated walk through six chapters of the mill's life — its founding, its village, its centuries of milling, its preservation, its present, and a timeline by which the visitor may locate themselves within the long arc of the building.

Chapter OneI.

Origins of the Mill

1810 – 1830

The mill rises in the years immediately preceding the War of 1812 on a stone foundation laid into the bank of the outflow from what was then called Beverley Pond. Its builder of record is Abel Stevens, a Loyalist who had walked north from Vermont two decades earlier in search of a country less hostile to the Crown. The hamlet he chose — first called Stone Mills, later Beverley, finally Delta — was scarcely twenty years old.

The structure is a textbook example of an Oliver Evans automatic mill, designed so that the grain, once delivered to the lower floor, would be lifted, cleaned, milled, sifted, and barrelled by an integrated arrangement of elevators, conveyors and hoppers — a single millwright, in principle, attending to the whole operation. The walls were laid in dressed limestone quarried within sight of the doorway. The original wooden window headers, still partly preserved beneath later brickwork, bear the marks of axe and adze.

Within a decade the building had passed from Stevens' partnership into the hands of William Jones, a Brockville merchant whose family would dominate the commerce of the upper St. Lawrence for two generations. Jones modernised the gearing, added a sawmill to the millrace, and made the mill the commercial centre of the village.

Chapter TwoII.

The Village of Delta

1796 – Present
Founding community
of the Rideau Lakes
Plate V  ·  King Streetc. 1905–present

The Old Town Hall, looking toward the mill along King Street — the civic and commercial heart of a village built around the waterwheel.

Before there was a mill there was a road, and before the road, a portage. The narrow neck of land between Upper and Lower Beverley Lakes had been used as a crossing place since long before European arrival, and the first settlers — most of them Loyalists displaced by the American Revolution — established a hamlet here in the closing years of the eighteenth century, christening it Stone Mills for the buildings that soon defined it.

By the eighteen-thirties the village had a tavern, a forge, a tannery, a school, two churches, and a half-dozen shops radiating from the millyard. It was the agricultural and commercial centre of the surrounding township, and would remain so until the railway, in the late nineteenth century, pulled the centre of gravity elsewhere. Renamed Delta in 1857 to avoid confusion with another Stone Mills in the colony, it remains today the oldest community in the Township of Rideau Lakes.

The mill, the Old Town Hall, the Denaut Mansion and the Blacksmith Shop together form a coherent ensemble of the village's first century — a streetscape, remarkably preserved, of the Upper Canadian agrarian economy.

Chapter ThreeIII.

Milling Through the Centuries

1810 – 1960
Stones · turbines
Red Fife wheat
1861 Denaut converts
the mill from
waterwheel to
Swain turbine

For its first half-century the mill was driven by a breastshot waterwheel, water falling from the head pond into wooden buckets and turning a shaft of oak that ran the entire ground floor. The power was distributed through direct-coupled wooden gearing to a single run of granite millstones imported from the Cevennes by way of Montreal.

In the early eighteen-sixties Walter Denaut invested some twenty thousand dollars — an enormous sum — in modernising the works. Two cast-iron Swain turbines, perhaps the only pair installed in the province, replaced the waterwheel. The wooden gearing gave way to belt-and-pulley drives, and the mill, now capable of running three and four runs of stones simultaneously, became the largest custom and merchant mill in the upper Rideau watershed.

The grain it ground, almost from the beginning, was the variety we now know as Red Fife — Canada's earliest distinct strain of bread wheat, named for the Peterborough farmer David Fife who first selected it in 1842. The variety thrived in the cold soils of Eastern Ontario, and the Old Stone Mill was, for several decades, one of the principal mills in which it was made into flour.

Chapter FourIV.

Preservation & Restoration

1963 – 2010
Society founded · NHSC
$2,000,000 invested
Plate IX  ·  Before restoration1965
Plate X  ·  After restoration2010

The mill ceased commercial operation in 1960. Within three years the building, now without an owner willing to maintain it, was visibly failing — roof sagging, parapet stones working loose, the wooden interior structure swollen with rain and pulling away from the walls. A group of local citizens, alarmed at what was happening, gathered at the Old Town Hall on a November evening in 1963 and incorporated the Delta Mill Society.

The work of preservation, then as now, fell almost entirely to volunteers. Over the following four decades the Society rebuilt the roof in three campaigns, repointed the limestone walls, reconstructed the floors, the husk and the gearing, and — most ambitiously — restored a full run of stones to operating order. The Old Stone Mill was declared a National Historic Site of Canada in 1970, one of the first private heritage buildings to receive the designation.

By the bicentenary in 2010, the Society had invested some two million dollars in restoration and conservation, drawn almost entirely from donations, fundraising and the work of more than a thousand individual volunteers across half a century.

Chapter FiveV.

The Mill Today

2026
61st operating season
Open Victoria Day – Labour Day
Living Heritage

A working museum

The mill operates as a public museum throughout the summer season. Heritage Red Fife wheat is grown locally and ground on the original millstones on scheduled milling days. Visitors may take home a paper sack of stone-ground flour.

The Forge

The Blacksmith Shop

Adjacent to the mill, the Society's coal-fired Blacksmith Shop demonstrates the heritage art of ironworking — the trade that built and maintained the mill itself, whose wrought-iron nails of 1810 are still in the building.

Community

The Old Town Hall

The former municipal hall, also in the Society's care, serves the Village of Delta as a community space — host to concerts of the Festival of Small Halls, local events, and the annual Delta Harvest Festival each September.

Chapter SixVI.

A Historical Timeline

1796 – 2026
Two centuries
at a glance
1796
First Loyalist settlement
Abel Stevens and a small company of Loyalist families settle the portage between Upper and Lower Beverley Lakes, establishing what will become Stone Mills.
1810 – 11
The mill is built
Construction of the Old Stone Mill in dressed local limestone, built on the Oliver Evans fully automated plan — the most advanced milling design of the era.
1830s
William Jones era
The mill passes to the Brockville merchant Jones family; a sawmill and carding works are added to the millrace.
1850
Walter Denaut acquires the mill
Denaut, a wealthy Delta merchant, purchases the mill and clears its three mortgages, beginning the most ambitious period of investment in the mill's history.
1861
Twin Swain turbines installed
Denaut invests some $20,000 — perhaps half a million in today's currency — replacing the waterwheel with two cast-iron turbines.
1870s
First photographs
Roderick Denaut photographs the mill from the pond — the earliest surviving image of the building.
1960
Commercial milling ceases
After 150 years, the mill closes as a commercial operation. The dam is removed; the building begins a slow decline into disrepair.
1963
Delta Mill Society founded
A volunteer society is incorporated to preserve the building and present the heritage of pre-industrial milling to future generations.
1970
National Historic Site of Canada
The Old Stone Mill is designated a National Historic Site — among the earliest privately owned buildings to receive the honour.
2010
Bicentenary
Two hundred years of the mill, marked by the substantial completion of half a century of restoration work and more than $2 million invested.
2026
Sixty-first season
The Society's 61st operating season opens on the Victoria Day weekend. The Delta Harvest Festival returns in September — milling demonstrations, blacksmithing, and community in a living heritage setting.
